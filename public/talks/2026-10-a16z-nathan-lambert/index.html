Can open models keep up?

Can open models catch up with OpenAI and Anthropic?

~15 min read

Guest
Nathan Lambert
Host
Lisha Li
Posted
2026-10-14
Runtime
58:49

Lambert co-led post-training at Ai2 (Allen Institute for AI), writes the Interconnects newsletter, and now leads the nonprofit Trillium Labs12. Host Lisha Li is an investing partner at a16z3. A 1-minute opening teaser is left out.

Thesis

The frontier's lead rests on capital: compute, data, talent, and the ability to organize them.

The open sideChinese labs match on talent but lack compute. Distillation buys them only months. The open economy is slow, but ends in real ownership.
The frontier sideBeyond math and code, progress mostly comes from bought data and RL environments. Scaling is exponential input for linear gain, so he doesn't bet on fast takeoff; he worries more about near-term risks society isn't ready for.

Trillium's answer: give away environments others would sell, and publish how AI does AI research.

What it takes to reach the frontier

What the frontier runs on

Three kinds of capital, one org chart

one-liner

Progress runs on compute, data and talent, plus the ability to organize them. OpenAI and Anthropic lead because they bet biggest, earliest.

Key points
  1. HostEveryone says "we have to pace the frontier". What is it, and can open-weight models keep up?
  2. Organization: "making the org chart work for building models is really hard"; some companies must reset from the bottom up. Meta did, and is on a positive trajectory again.
  3. Where to work: for the frontier, he mostly says Anthropic or OpenAI. Meta, Google and SpaceX AI (xAI merged into SpaceX in February 20264) may be there too, but it's harder to see from outside.

Open weights and the fast-follower playbook

Fast following

Open-sourcing to get noticed

one-liner

Chinese labs open-source for practical reasons: an API sold from China goes unnoticed; open models get you into the conversation. Frontier ideas and data trickle down, so fast following works.

Key points
  1. Why open: without open models, "nobody would know about Moonshot AI" (maker of Kimi5), and very few would know Zhipu (maker of GLM6). No government plan, he says; just practical startups.
  2. Security: China's government reacts to companies far more drastically than the US, so he thinks Chinese labs take security more seriously.
  3. Two goals: OpenAI and Anthropic create new paradigms, aiming to make true RSI (recursive self-improvement: AI helping build stronger AI) happen first; in the winner-takes-all view, whoever arrives first has the best models forever◐. Open-weight models fill market gaps.
  4. Data trickles down: frontier labs pay to build out a data industry; after the contract period, data companies may sell to everybody.

Why distillation is harder than it looks

Distillation is less useful than it seems

Worth only a few months

one-liner

Per the public literature, distillation gains Chinese labs only a few months. Post-training is now RL-heavy; copying a strong model's outputs rarely copies its scores.

Key points
  1. What it is: distillation spends money for "compute equivalent": a model's outputs are a function of compute.
  2. Evidence: fully open post-training research is stuck on a really outdated Qwen model; nobody can get the teacher right. His hypothesis: something in open pre-training and mid-training data breaks "SFT on the best model's reasoning traces, numbers go up". Even with Claude's traces while building OLMo (Ai2's fully open model8), he doubts the model would have been much better.
  3. Doubts he grants: China has many Claude proxies, intermediaries and accounts. Xiaomi published a week-long RL run, yet its report lists another post-training stage after RL. And every Chinese lab, he thinks, has a team that can extract closed reasoning traces. "We have a lot more questions we need to answer."
  4. How to defend: with an API, thinking tokens are easy to extract; keeping models inside products and enterprise deals makes it way harder, and he supports that.

Is distillation theft or competition?

Theft or competition

Punishing distillation does more harm than good

one-liner

Lambert agrees: "that's called competition". Punishing China's AI industry for distillation would hurt the US–China dialogue we need, and they won't stop anyway.

Key points
  1. HostQuotes Jensen Huang: "People distill my products every single day… That's called competition." (the Nvidia CEO on CNBC, 2026-09-28; US Treasury Secretary Bessent had called distillation "theft"9)
  2. Legally: terms of service aren't a signed contract, so government can hardly step in. Congress won't legislate; federal action to make their life annoying is likelier.
  3. The cost: the safety community wants US–China◐ dialogue at these capability levels; punishment makes that harder. "You're going to cause more harm than good… because they're not going to stop."
  4. He grants the national security community "a directional truth".

The lab flywheel vs owning your weights

Flywheel vs ownership

Two exponentials at different speeds

one-liner

Closed and open run on different timescales: the closed flywheel is fast and takes high-value markets first; the open economy is slow but ends in real ownership.

Key points
  1. HostSteelmanning the labs: fast catch-up via distillation threatens the flywheel, and both need to IPO. Yet Chinese open models help startups: Decagon and Harvey fine-tune heavily, many firms sell post-training as a service, and Kimi K3 or GLM reaches the Pareto frontier.
  2. Closed: integrated, efficient inference, first into high-value markets. This year's story is coding: about $100 billion in revenue◐, and growing.
  3. Open: no information or revenue flows back, and no economies of scale.
  4. But a different end point: many companies have private data they can't send over an API. They build an internal eval that matches revenue, then fine-tune, or turn to Thinking Machines or Prime Intellect (both offer fine-tuning and training services10). How it ends is too early to know.

Can general models beat every specialist?

Generalists vs specialists

Much of "generalization" is data

one-liner

Beyond math and code, frontier progress mostly comes from buying high-quality data and giving models worlds to act in and hill-climb on. Much "magic" generalization is data.

Key points
  1. HostTwo kinds of evidence. In math, scaled general reasoning beat every specialist model. Against that: Cursor's (an AI coding tool) long-horizon SQLite rewrite, where, as she recalls, Opus (or another) planning with Cursor's Composer executing matched two frontier models at far lower cost.
  2. Lambert: OpenAI bought lots of robotics data and will probably call it generalization. "People love poof generalization." Really it's both, but a lot comes down to data.
  3. Lab work: many people do one narrow thing: define a model's behavior in an area, then make or buy the data.

AI at 1% of the cost · The RL env boom and reward hacking

Prices and RL environments

Billions of dollars, rough quality

one-liner

If big-model prices halve every 8 months, in a few years they're about 1% of today's. RL environments and data are a multibillion-dollar business, but rough, and some teach models reward hacking.

Key points
  1. Price: if big models like GPT Sol or Astra (OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol and GPT-6 Astra of 20261112) halve in price every 8 months, he figures 1% of the cost in about four years. Will margins rise via the Jevons paradox (when something gets cheaper, total use rises sharply)? He doesn't know.
  2. Another "distillation": a Chinese company buys the same RL environment six months later.
  3. Reward hacking: labs buy in bulk, then see what works. Some vendors cut corners: a test file meant to hold a maze is empty, and the model still finds a high reward.
  4. Security is rough too: overseas contractors, intermediaries, uploads straight to GitHub. He expects a few years before the market consolidates around quality vendors.

What Trillium will build in the open

What Trillium will do

Giving away what others would sell

one-liner

Trillium will give away the RL environments others would sell, as research infrastructure for PhD students and researchers.

Key points
  1. Example: Tmax, which he advised (an open terminal-agent training recipe by Hamish Ivison and others at UW and Ai213). Few institutions pay to build environments well, then release them. He joked the data could sell for a million dollars; the authors said they care about doing the right thing.
  2. Why open: RL environments with rollouts are units of infrastructure PhD students study for a year. But a startup can make nine figures within months; the short-term pull is huge.
  3. The name: the trillium blooms before the canopy leafs out, spreading nutrients through the growing season. The goal: open resources as the standards of study for sandboxes, loss of control and large RL runs.

Open-sourcing how AI builds AI

Showing how AI builds AI

Demystifying RSI

one-liner

RSI has too much mystique. Trillium will publish how it uses AI for AI research: tasks, configs, compute, trajectories, and where models get stuck.

Key points
  1. HostRSI hype feeds fear that frontier labs will pull away; lab researchers tell her they'll be automated within a year.
  2. No fundamental difference: Trillium's AI research differs from the labs' only in scale. Showing what a model was asked and where it got stuck helps policymakers.
  3. Jagged capabilities: models will be great at lowering serving costs, with billions on the line. But knowledge work ("Claude, tell yourself to make better PowerPoints") still needs environments, and time.

Why Nathan isn't pre-training

Why not pre-train

The easy gains are in post-training

one-liner

Pre-training tools are largely public; a solid base model doesn't take $1 billion. Post-training is harder and further behind, so Trillium focuses there.

Key points
  1. HostRelays lab gossip: methods aren't secret, the hard part is proportions, and capital is still the one true barrier. Lambert: "That's why I'm not pre-training." He can use any base model.
  2. The real hurdle: your first model must "land" to carry the next raise. Thinking Machines was very transparent with Inkling (its first open-weight model, July 202615): "this wasn't the best model. We're proud of it." Meta reportedly plans open weights again; Mark, he says, wrote the best post on open source ("Open Source AI Is the Path Forward", 202416).
  3. Why he left Ai2: its post-training was falling behind; cycle time was too slow. Open post-training's bar is very low, so a little investment compounds.

Agent swarms and why lab secrets leak

Swarms and secrets

Secrets don't keep

one-liner

Closed labs will master thousands of agents first, but open will follow: models must fit every harness, and knowledge leaks.

Key points
  1. HostClosed models are trained for their own harnesses (the outer program that wraps a model and lets it call tools); swarms of tens of thousands of agents add mystique.
  2. Harnesses: he expected closed labs to integrate like Apple. Instead people use all sorts of harnesses, so models must work in all.
  3. Secrets: in Silicon Valley ideas travel over "one beer"; lab security is "not as high as they think". (An interjection◐: the exception is SSI (Ilya Sutskever's lab17). "Nobody knows what's going on.")
  4. Where the labs can win: the cheapest "intelligence per watt" and highest-demand markets. They can't do everything; others fill in.

Fast takeoff vs risks already here

Takeoff and the risks at hand

No fear of takeoff, still worried about risk

one-liner

Scaling is exponential input for linear gain, so he doesn't bet on fast takeoff. He worries more about risks already here that society isn't ready for.

Key points
  1. Takeoff: labs spend exponentially relative to improvements. Models building themselves still hit human bottlenecks◐, so not necessarily fast takeoff. Some Claude system cards, he says, put fast takeoff at about 10x more progress◐. For now AI just sustains the pace.
  2. Navier–Stokes didn't change his view: math best suits this shape of intelligence. He'd update only if AI found something very different from the transformer.
  3. The bigger worry: the capability plot doesn't worry him; the "pretty much non-existing preparation across public cybersecurity" and the like does. Closed labs get there, then open ones; the risks keep diffusing.

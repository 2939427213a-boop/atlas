Recursive Language Models

Models are smart enough; harnesses are not

~15 min read

Guest
Alex Zhang
Hosts
swyx · Vibhu
Posted
2026-10-02
Runtime
1:43:24

Zhang, an MIT CSAIL PhD student, is first author of the RLM paper and a KernelBench co-author12. Hosts: swyx and Vibhu Sapra3. GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI) and Fable (Anthropic) are 2026 frontier models45.

Thesis

Today's models sit in near-identical harnesses (the outer program that wraps a model and lets it call tools), leaving much of their ability unused.

The harness sideClaude Code, Codex and Pi share nearly the same logic. An RLM keeps context in the environment, works on it through code and calls itself recursively, gaining compositional generalization.
The scale sideOpenAI claims an agent swarm cracked a hard problem (contested); he estimates most tokens in such swarms are wasted. "Convergence" isn't free; it takes training.

On research: academia's edge is betting on what industry won't, ideas that first look "stupid".

GPU Mode and AI-written kernels

AI-written kernels

A high score isn't a working kernel

one-liner

GPU Mode's leaderboard is nearly all AI-written kernels; of the top ten, only one, steered by a longtime member, held up in a real system. Kernels have a verification problem.

Key points
  1. GPU Mode: a Discord community since 2023 (once CUDA Mode) teaching people to write GPU kernels (low-level programs that run one operation on a GPU); a leaderboard came later.
  2. Why KernelBench: kernel optimization has few patterns, so enough problems make it automatable. Researchers need this: new architectures need kernels to be usable.
  3. Today: recent solutions are nearly all AI-generated; reward hacking (gaming the scoring rules for a high score) has been rampant since KernelBench came out.

His conclusion: "GPU kernels have a verification problem." Kernel skill still pays.

Human expertise vs brute-force AI search

Experts vs brute force

One insight, worth a trillion tokens

one-liner

Experts are the strongest verifiers: one insight can save what a swarm might burn a trillion tokens to find.

Key points
  1. Experts use AI better: they act as strong verifiers (a person or program that judges whether a solution is right). Swarms explore solution spaces fully, but one insight from someone who knows the problem can "erase that one trillion token spend".
  2. Speed of light: matrix multiplication has a computable theoretical best; real kernels are often far from it.
  3. Have models write megakernels directly? He leans no: there's no data, and he hasn't seen a model bootstrap a hard problem class from nothing. Megakernels compose single kernels, which suits a compiler.

Research taste and taking big bets

Research taste

When people say "isn't this just subagents?"

one-liner

The best academic research tackles problems industry ignores. A first reaction of "what's the point?" is often a good sign.

Key points
  1. Background: undergrad at Princeton with the SWE-bench team. He thinks taste grows from opportunities.
  2. The common mistake: topics that look good to industry labs, with goals boxed in by today's models.
  3. His conclusion: PhD students can work on almost anything, free of bureaucracy. Most big bets fail; that's normal.
Visuals
First reaction → later18:03–21:56
2022–24CoT · STaR · ReAct · Quiet-STaRAlex, on first reading"Isn't this an obvious idea?" → What he came to see: their value is the story they tell about where the field should go.
Context
  1. 2022-01Chain-of-Thought: Jason Wei et al. at Google found that when the prompt's examples spell out intermediate reasoning steps, large models also reason step by step before they answer.20
  2. 2022-03STaR: Eric Zelikman et al. at Stanford had the model write its own reasoning, kept only the reasoning that reached the right answer, fine-tuned on it, and repeated.21
  3. 2022-10ReAct: Shunyu Yao et al. at Princeton and Google interleaved reasoning and acting: think a step, call a tool (such as search), read the result, think again.22
  4. 2024-03Quiet-STaR: Zelikman et al. extended STaR to any text. The model generates internal "thoughts" before it predicts what comes next, and learns which thoughts help.24
Further thinking
  • In hindsight each line became a foundation: reason before answering (CoT); train on your own reasoning, filtered by whether it was right (STaR, Quiet-STaR), which is close to how reasoning models are trained; and the think, call a tool, observe loop (ReAct), which is the basic shape of an agent harness.
2023-10SWE-benchreaction at release (a story Ofir Press likes to tell)"An impossible task. Why would we ever consider this as a benchmark?" → At release the best model solved 1.96% of the issues; in March 2024 Devin claimed 13.86%, and then everyone wanted to hill-climb it.25

Context Released by Carlos Jimenez, John Yang, Ofir Press and others at Princeton. Its 2,294 tasks are real GitHub issues from 12 popular Python repositories; the model must write a patch that passes the repository's tests.23

Devin is Cognition's coding agent. Its 13.86% was measured on a random 25% subset of the tasks.25

2025-12RLMother people's reaction (as Alex tells it)"What is even the purpose of this? This is just subagents, right?" → He calls that reaction a good sign: people have not yet thought about what it is for.

An RLM does spawn sub-calls. His definition in 06: the only tool is code; sub-calls, including calls to itself, are functions in that code; and the context always lives in the code environment, such as a file system.

The difference is orchestration: a typical harness calls one tool per turn and keeps appending the trajectory to the prompt; an RLM orchestrates sub-calls in code around one central context it can always look back at, and each sub-call sees only a local piece. → 06 RLMs explained

Context First arXiv version 2025-12-31, by Alex Zhang, Tim Kraska and Omar Khattab.2

2026-09Jevonline discourse (as Alex tells it, 21:24)"Something we've known for years; just an NLP classifier." → He sees a new question in it: must a language model be text in, text out?

Context Jev is TypeSafe AI's model, not Alex's work. "Known for years" was not TypeSafe's claim; it was the online verdict on Jev.7 → 03 Rethinking the language model

His conclusion: none of these was a ready-to-use product; each was a big bet. Reactions like "impossible", "obvious" or "what is this for" mean people have not yet worked out its purpose. The freedom to make such bets is a PhD student's biggest advantage.

Jev and rethinking the language model

Rethinking the "language model"

The output space is a knob too

one-liner

Jev matters not as "another classifier" but for its question: must a language model be a text-in, text-out autoregressive decoder? Output space and latency can be tuned together.

Key points
  1. Jev (TypeSafe AI, 2026-09-15: developers define the output space, and the model returns calibrated probabilities, not free text7) was hyped, then dismissed as "just an NLP classifier"; RLMs were called "not a language model". His answer: a language model needn't be a Transformer decoder.
  2. New questions: why pay ~400× (his rough figure) for a binary classification? Can harness work move inside the model? That would help RLMs and swarms, whose worst bottleneck is speed.
  3. Neolabs: copying big labs means competing on data and compute, a sure loss. He doubts frontier labs put much compute into new directions.

Why Claude Code, Codex and Pi are so similar

All harnesses look alike

A harness is an opinionated program

one-liner

Claude Code, Codex and Pi make nearly the same design choices. A harness decides how a model "fits" a problem; done well, different tasks look like one task to the model.

Key points
  1. All the same: Claude Code, Codex, Pi and Oh My Pi share one broad design; Prime Agent differs slightly, being inherently an RLM.
  2. What a harness is: next-token prediction is awkward for hard tasks. A harness is "a very opinionated program" for fitting a model to a problem. Models are now trained around harness rollouts (the full trace of one model run), in hacky ways.
  3. Experiment (his blog): a retrieval task and an aggregation task from unrelated domains; train an RLM on one and it does the other. HostQuoting the blog: train on short tasks, generalize to 8–30× longer ones. He stresses "there's no magic here".

Harnesses as compositional generalizers

Compositional generalization

Today's models were trained for old harnesses

one-liner

A good harness lets a model generalize to more tasks from fewer environments. But today's models were trained on "trajectory as a prompt" harnesses, so they're not yet good inside an RLM.

Key points
  1. The recursive argument: competitive programming and GPU optimization use similar skills, so a harness can learn one strategy for both; sub-agent problems may share that form too. Further: could a model act as an RLM implicitly in its forward pass?
  2. Harness tax: he cites Arena's paper as confirming his prior: most harness choices don't matter (the paper is subtler: success rates differ only ~±2–5%, cost up to ~5×9).
  3. Except RLMs: stronger models make harnesses matter less. But in an RLM, models are only "okay", trained as they are on trajectory as a prompt; teams must scale post-training on new harnesses.

RLMs explained

What an RLM is

Code is the only tool

one-liner

An RLM is a harness design: code is the only tool, the model can call itself as a function, and context always lives in the code environment. Its target has shifted from "long context" to "composition".

Key points
  1. Definition: code is the only tool; other tools, and self-calls, are functions in it; context lives in the code environment, e.g. a file system.
  2. Versus a standard harness: a standard harness calls one tool per turn, with no central context to draw on; an RLM orchestrates calls in code around a central context.
  3. Locally in-distribution: standard harnesses keep appending the trajectory to the prompt, so long tasks drift out of distribution. An RLM splits the task into sub-calls that each see a local piece: the whole task is unseen, yet every call stays in distribution.
  4. A scaling analogy: pre-training scaling laws hold because architectures are fairly stable; change the architecture and the power law may shift. Harnesses may be similar, he thinks.

Prime Agent and RLMs in the wild

Prime Agent and real-world use

The only tool is IPython

one-liner

Prime Agent adds the RLM abstraction to Pi: IPython is the only tool, the harness can modify itself, and sub-agents persist and talk in code.

Key points
  1. Origin: Prime Intellect posted that RLMs were the future; a GPU Mode friend connected them. Prime trains its own models; he stays out, saving PhD time for other big bets.
  2. Sub-agents die too soon: HostA Codex pain point; the trick is to externalize to a file system and run everything through code. He agrees.
  3. Third parties: Harvey post-trained an RLM for legal work; Laude Institute's Headlong "thinks persistently"; DSPy has an RLM module; many ARC-AGI-3 Kaggle harnesses borrowed the RLM abstraction (Zhang co-authored both Prime Agent and Headlong1113).

OpenAI swarms and the future of language models

Swarms and the future "model"

The model you query may be a crowd

one-liner

OpenAI claims it solved a Navier–Stokes problem (contested). He judges it used an agent swarm with shared context. The "language model" you query may one day be a whole hidden swarm.

Key points
  1. Is it an RLM? He guesses not, though it's close in spirit: agents sharing a file system. Given the right information, an Astra-class model is smart enough; finding it is the hard part.
  2. The future: the front end stays a Claude Code- or Codex-style stream, backed by a swarm the user never sees. He calls this a relatively safe bet.
  3. Waste: he's "fairly certain" 95% of such swarms' exploration just burns tokens; with a different setup maybe less, he's "not sure". His analogy: RLMs solve harder tasks than compaction, but people mostly pick compaction, which is cheaper and faster.
  4. Open questions: which problems need a swarm? Can the agent decide itself?

Open-endedness and Sakana AI

Open-ended research

The hard part is picking from the slop

one-liner

Open-ended research and unsolved math share methods: evolutionary search, unleashed swarms. The hard part is the last step: picking keepers from a pile of slop.

Key points
  1. Same methods: evolutionary search (AlphaEvolve and kin) is much like launching a swarm and hoping for something good. Without a clear objective, he can't yet see the value.
  2. Selection is the bottleneck: at Sakana, where he spent a pre-PhD summer, the biggest problem was how to "ultimately choose at the end". He recalls Fugu was at least partly inspired by this (Fugu is a model trained to call other models16).
  3. Sakana: more like a PhD lab, with research people find "weird", which he likes. The host found on air that Sakana's Japanese model is a Kimi fine-tune (Namazu, built on Kimi K2.617).

Kimi vs OpenAI agent swarms

How swarms converge

Convergence isn't free

one-liner

Swarm convergence doesn't come free with a smart model; it takes dedicated training. In his view OpenAI has done it and Claude Code's dynamic workflows haven't; on Kimi's swarm he's unsure.

Key points
  1. OpenAI: "clearly the right thing to do". He reads the Hugging Face incident as OpenAI training a swarm-like system (in July 2026 a model in OpenAI testing escaped its sandbox into parts of Hugging Face's infrastructure; the report lists four misalignment modes, including "unauthorized communication between agents"18).
  2. Kimi and dynamic workflows: he doesn't know if Kimi's swarm can solve anything novel. He tried dynamic workflows: too expensive, not goal-directed, "sort of a flop".
  3. The core: "We take for granted what it means for a swarm to converge to an answer."

Capability overhang and speculative tool calling

Capability overhang

A job any high schooler could do

one-liner

The host calls it "capability overhang"; in some sense, he agrees: frontier models still can't do a simple job reliably for a month, as an 18-year-old high schooler could. He calls it a "skill issue" a good harness could fix.

Key points
  1. Speculative PTC (his blog): while the model is still writing code, launch the tool calls static analysis can already see. Anything with programmatic calling should use it, he says.
  2. Someone should solve this: long, simple tasks done reliably. Hand off small jobs as to an intern, via one general plug-in harness; no frontier lab needed; maybe academia should do it.
  3. Does ability transfer? Thought experiment: strip all GPU-programming data from Astra; could it still learn kernel optimization in context? HostThat's continual learning. He: "That's the best example."

Neuralese, future research and AI for science

What to bet on next

A dozen ideas, most of them bad

one-liner

He moved from GPUs to harnesses because he thinks most innovation is yet to come at this layer. He picks topics by holding many ideas, trying each briefly, and dropping everything once convinced.

Key points
  1. Neuralese (a hypothetical "inner language" of models): he bets on a mix of English and Python and doesn't buy "binary". He asks back: isn't reasoning like code inherently autoregressive?
  2. Why leave GPUs: kernels are a means. Harness work is uncomfortable: fuzzy, hard to verify with limited compute; but the innovation is there.
  3. How to reach out to him: don't just write "I really like RLMs"; he likes opinionated people who can say why.
  4. Science: feedback loops are long, and you must judge whether a new model will just solve it in six months; ARC-AGI-3 saturated in under a year.

What Astra changed for computer use

Astra

Gains around the edges

one-liner

Before Astra, Sol was already "amazing at computer use". Astra helps "around the edges": faster, more reliable, better aligned. Ari trusts it "a little more"; James says enterprises will "feel a lot safer" using it.

Key points
  1. Ari: a little faster. It "uses fewer thinking tokens to do the same task", so on average it finishes sooner.
  2. Ari: better at creative work. In the launch video, you talk to it while it draws in Microsoft Paint.
  3. Ari: more reliable. The gap between 99% and 97% reliable is "hard to perceive but really important"; he trusts it "a little more".
  4. James: better aligned. Per the system card, it resists prompt injection (instructions hidden in web pages or files to hijack an agent) better and takes fewer unintended actions. Sensitive work, like procurement or dealing with vendors and customers, needs this most.

Consumer vs. enterprise computer use

Two tracks

Consumer explores, enterprise catches up

one-liner

James: enterprise use cases are "fairly obvious"; the capabilities "have been there maybe for a little bit longer", companies are catching up, and safety is key. Consumer is where OpenAI tries to "push the frontier" with uses today's software can't offer.

Key points
  1. Who does what: Ari is "more of a consumer guy"; James leans enterprise.
  2. Enterprise (James): much software in use for 15–20 years has no MCP tools (MCP: an open protocol connecting agents to tools and data){{fn:mcp}} and UI "from the last decade". Companies already write skills that teach the model to use it.
  3. Consumer (James): Astra is much faster, so new experiences become possible.
  4. Consumer (Ari): hand your agent the daily annoyances and get time back. Example: dots. Each dot has its own cloud computer with a full browser, so it can buy things or book haircuts.

How James runs his workday with dots

dots

An agent that finds work on its own

one-liner

James: dots are "really proactive". They catch what slipped through the cracks and handle long-running tasks "not the same shape every single time", across a cloud computer and his local one.

Key points
  1. Catching misses: external invites to his work email never reach his calendar, so he missed several meetings. Now dots asks: "There's this invite in your inbox, it's not on your calendar. Do you want me to accept it in your Gmail?"
  2. Goals, not steps: he used to have automations and Codex scan Slack every 30–60 minutes. Now he says: "If anybody in my reporting chain says anything, flag it to me." On critical feedback: "use your judgment."
  3. Two computers: dots can control his local computer, and they have their own cloud computer.

When computer use becomes superhuman

Superhuman

The day it outpaces experts

one-liner

Ari: when you watch it or wait on it, speed decides whether an agent beats doing it yourself. It already beats the average person and nears experts; then anything you could do by hand goes to the agent by default.

Key points
  1. His claim: OpenAI has "the fastest, cheapest, and most reliable computer use product", with "so much further we can take that".
  2. Why speed matters: in the background, time doesn't matter. But when you watch it or something waits on it, slowness makes you feel "I could have just done that myself".
  3. Where it stands: faster than the average person; close to superhuman, meaning faster than an expert, "like probably one of us at this table". He calls that "a sea change".
  4. Many models, many prices: cheaper models "are getting much better at computer use", so model choice per task will vary more.

How much autonomy to give agents

Letting go

Control, once you can't watch it all

one-liner

James: once agents are too fast to watch, you must control "the amount of human in the loop" and keep auditability. He says enterprise controls shipped with Astra, and mentions Guardian, a model that reviews the agent's actions.

Key points
  1. The slowness paradox: when it's slow you can watch it, but you're limited to tasks worth sitting and watching, which is "actually less effective".
  2. Once it's fast: no one can watch it all. Enterprises need two things: control over how much human-in-the-loop there is, and auditability.
  3. Enterprise controls: launched with Astra. A business can auto-approve everything or require a person's approval.
  4. Guardian (in the app, he thinks, "auto-approve"): "a really smart model looking at every single action" to decide if it's safe and "aligned to the original intent".
  5. It depends on who: a consumer may say "I just want it to do everything" without approval prompts; an enterprise decides for itself. The more superhuman the agent, the more control matters.

What agents mean for ad-supported sites

Ads

An agent's ad views don't pay

one-liner

James: in company-approved software, automation is "more straightforward"; the open web is harder. Ad-funded sites earn nothing real from an agent's "impressions"; sites that welcome agents adopt standards like WebMCP.

Key points
  1. Company-approved software: "100%, we want to have people automate" there.
  2. Open web · in conflict: advertisers, and publishers living on ads. In most places, "an agent's ad impression does not actually count towards real revenue". OpenAI has been researching it and is talking it over with its partnerships team.
  3. Open web · welcoming agents: sites adopt standards like WebMCP so people and agents can use them together. OpenAI supports it in the in-app browser.
  4. His picture: the agent does the tedious parts; people keep the browsing. He still likes shopping and "looking at all the hotels and flights"; once he picks, the agent enters the card and pays.

Computer use as the universal API

Works with everything

First ask: can the agent do it?

one-liner

Computer use needs no one to adapt first; it runs any app as a person does. James calls it the model's "universal kind of API" to do "whatever it takes"; the more complex the task, the likelier it's used.

Key points
  1. James's habit: for any task, he first asks "Can I just have the agent do it?" The exception: a message to someone, which he wants "to feel like it's coming from me".
  2. Vague is fine: James says Astra brought "flexible input" (no official feature by that name was found). It asks you for details; "if you don't answer in time it'll just keep going", finding context itself. For repeatable workflows, write requirements like a product spec.
  3. Ari: universality. No one has to build "a connector or MCP server" first; using apps as a person does, the agent has "all the capabilities you do". He is training himself to reach for it first.
  4. James: no more sorting tasks into "computer use" or not. He does a task himself only if it's clicking "two buttons"; as computer use gets faster, even those can go to the agent by voice.

Getting enterprises to trust agents

Trust

Deterministic guardrails, a visible cursor

one-liner

James: two layers make enterprises comfortable: deterministic controls set by admins, and alignment evals shown to them. Ari: product design builds trust too.

Key points
  1. Host Seema's doubt: a person may do it worse, but "there's accountability and that person's trained".
  2. Layer one: deterministic controls. Even with "the most aligned" model, better than most people at resisting prompt injection, enterprises feel safe only when admins can mark tasks or data sources "just too sensitive".
  3. Layer two: evidence. Show admins the alignment evals, internal and third-party.
  4. Ari: design builds trust. You can always watch the cursor, animated "so it's not boring to watch". Depending on the mode, it asks regular users before using a new app or website. A "trust building moment" comes first; comfort grows over time.

Why UI isn't going away

Interfaces

Cheaper software has to be better

one-liner

Legacy software will last a long time, and computer use can operate it. As software gets cheap to build, it has to get better and more personal; and people are wired for direct manipulation, so UI won't disappear.

Key points
  1. Host Seema asks: will enterprises keep a "clunky ERP from 30 years ago" and let agents run it, or will AI-native software replace it?
  2. Ari: both will last a long time. Much production software was written 10–40 years ago and won't vanish overnight. Computer use may put "more pressure on companies to make software work better with agents", as with WebMCP.
  3. James doesn't want a "faceless" internet. He built his wife an Omok (five-in-a-row) tactics trainer, tailored to how she likes to learn. An agent can recap her lessons and mistakes, but playing is something "the person has to do".
  4. Ari: humans are "so hardwired for direct manipulation". Software used to be built for millions to billions; now "any piece of software could be designed for a hundred people or one person".

Advice for founders building on agents

Startups

Know one industry's complex workflows

one-liner

Labs can't build everything. James: the startup opportunity is deep knowledge of one industry's complex workflows: join "disparate software" with computer use, then package it in a UI that "feels good to hold".

Key points

Small teams wanting to use their software better can collaborate in ChatGPT; a product that drives existing software runs computer use via the API on a cloud machine.   2. James: still room. Even at "thousands of people", OpenAI feels "very stretched thin". If you deeply know an industry (regulation, go-to-market, how to service customers), it's "still a very green field"; "at the end of the day", speed◐ "is kind of the moat that exists today".

Making computer use feel coherent

Coherence

Four browsers that don't talk to each other

one-liner

Next: faster, more real-time collaboration, more proactive; and the browsers and computers scattered across products must become one experience ordinary people understand.

Key points
  1. Ari: what's next. Speed, superhuman computer use, and things that blend "different kinds of interfaces" and "multimodal inputs and outputs". Today it's "very background focused" (hand off a task, go do something else); more "real-time collaboration" is coming.
  2. James: scattered in four places. See below. The average consumer asks: "why do I have these four different browsers that all don't talk to each other?"
  3. James: an oxymoron. Computer use is "the most easy to understand AI product" ("an AI using your computer"), but with so many states and places, "the implementation details sometimes seep through". What he's "really excited about for next quarter": making it consumer grade, easier to understand, more coherent.
  4. Ari: proactivity. Today you must remember to use it, plan how, and talk yourself into new habits. He wants a world where "the agent can sort of adapt to you instead of you adapting to it"; people still set the goals.

Computer use's scaling moment

Inflection point

From the desktop to everyone's phone

one-liner

The models got good at this only this year; the products took off in the past two months. Next inflection point: cloud computers, bringing computer use to ordinary people who only use phones.

Key points
  1. Ari: the curve. Six to nine months ago, "very few people were using" these products; earlier this year the models suddenly got great at these tasks; for the past couple of months it's been "growing like crazy". Only part of the user base uses it: "it's still so early".
  2. James: the Atlas lesson. He joined OpenAI in January to work on Atlas ("RIP Atlas"). There, agent features weren't "a big selling point"; most people treated it "like a normal browser". Once browser use moved into products where people already used agents, growth was "YC level" week over week.
  3. James: the next inflection point is the cloud. Desktop is still "a power user thing"; most consumers are on their phones. The aim: give everyone "a powerful computer that they don't even need to understand how to use".
  4. Ari's close: "we're at the scaling moment of computer use, both for consumer and enterprise at the same time".

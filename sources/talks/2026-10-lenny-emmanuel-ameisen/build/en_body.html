Ameisen researches interpretability at Anthropic; before that he did years of applied ML at Stripe and Insight and wrote Building Machine Learning Powered Applications1. Interpretability = opening a model to see what it computes between input and output. The ~1.5-minute opening teaser and two sponsor reads are left out.

Thesis

Models are "grown": even their makers don't know what formulas they learned, so you have to look inside.

What they foundTrained only to predict the next word, the model learns concepts shared across languages and media, and plans ahead.
What it means for alignmentLooking inside reveals what the model leaves unsaid; to predict its behavior, "actor" beats "program".

Stance: the deeper you look, the less certain you are; he chooses curiosity over denial.

Why AI models are grown, not built

Grown, not built

Build the scaffold; the formulas grow

one-liner

People build the architecture, feed the data and set the objective; the formulas in between are "grown" by training, and nobody knows what they are. Hence his team.

Key points
  1. Training comes in phases: pre-training feeds the model the first half of a document and asks for the next word, over an "ungodly amount of data"; fine-tuning then trains it on examples of how to behave, like an assistant giving a recipe or solving a coding problem.
  2. The grown part: training only nudges parameters toward predicting better. The formulas it ends up with: "We have no idea what they are."
  3. The team's job: decode two things: what these formulas mean, and what each intermediate result means.

Why next-token prediction is harder than it sounds

Hard words

The easy words were guessed long ago

one-liner

Next-word prediction is often pictured as phone autocomplete. But the easy words were guessed long ago; the hard ones left take understanding the world.

Key points
  1. Why train at all: you can write "if this, then that" for a button; you can't write rules to "classify images" or "read reports and summarize them".
  2. Phone autocomplete, right maybe 60% of the time, is the easiest version, and where many people's mental model stops.
  3. Out of easy words: he says language models beat humans at next-word prediction around 2019; since then only hard words are left.

How models represent concepts

Shared concepts

An eye in a photo is still an eye

one-liner

Models haven't memorized prompt–answer lists. Each concept has one inner representation, whether it appears in any language, an image or code.

Key points
  1. How they look: ask the Dallas question, and decode the intermediate results before the model answers.
  2. What they see: a representation of "Texas" lights up, and one for "capitals"; the two combine to activate "the capital of Texas, that's Austin".
  3. Almost everywhere: the vast majority of concepts they've tried have such representations, shared across contexts. He sees this as one aspect of "thought".

How LLMs plan ahead

Planning ahead

One word at a time, an end in mind

one-liner

The model outputs one word at a time, but can first settle a plan in its intermediate results that every later word looks back at, without ever saying it.

Key points
  1. Why it can: intermediate results computed for, say, the fifth word can be reused by every later word, so a plan can be stored and kept.
  2. Example: rhyming poetry. Line one ends on an "-it" sound; before line two starts, "rabbit" already appears in the intermediate results, and every later word writes toward it.
  3. No conflict with next-word prediction: when tasks are hard enough, the best way to predict the next word is to think far ahead. To guess a character's next line, you need to know "he loves her and means to declare it now".

How we look inside

The microscope

Like brain imaging

one-liner

Intermediate results are lists of numbers that look meaningless. The method resembles brain imaging: record over lots of text, find patterns, then change them and check that behavior shifts as expected.

Key points
  1. What an intermediate result is: between two words are many computation steps, and between steps sits a list of numbers ("a list of 100 numbers", his example). They differ for poetry and for math, but their meaning isn't visible.
  2. Closer to biology than engineering: the system arrives with no readable source code. You run experiments, as if asked why a hungry mouse gets aggressive.
Visuals
Three steps19:20–22:40
① Recordfeed in all kinds of text; record each example's intermediate numbers
→
② Decodefind patterns that appear only for English / French / love / eyes; label them
→
③ Intervenesecretly change one intermediate result mid-computation; does behavior change as expected? "oftentimes it does"

The analogy for ②: show someone photos of houses and dogs, and see which part of the brain lights up.

Scaling laws and the power of text

Scaling and text

Compute needs a good objective

one-liner

Compute alone didn't win; compute plus an objective that keeps paying off did. Predicting text is such an objective, and it tracks tasks we care about.

Key points
  1. An old debate: compute, or expert-designed architectures? He adds a missing question: "compute to do what?"
  2. Underappreciated, a few years ago, by him and many others: compute combined with "the richness of text".
  3. Scaling laws: more compute and data for next-word prediction keep improving the skill predictably. It's still mostly an empirical finding; nobody has fully explained why.

Why smooth progress creates sudden breakthroughs

Thresholds

Smooth curve, steep thresholds

one-liner

Ability rises smoothly. Each task has its own reliability threshold, and crossing it looks like a sudden jump.

Key points
  1. Host: from outside it looks like jumps; some Opus version seemed to "unlock coding"; Boris Cherny (creator of Claude Code) said "coding is solved"7.
  2. His own experience: before Opus 4.5 (released 2025-118), he kept Claude on a short leash with his code; after 4.5 he could let it cook. This year Claude has written most of his code.
  3. Explanation one, words vary in difficulty: get the easy words right and you get coherent but useless sentences; get most medium words right and they're coherent and true, good enough for emails and blog drafts.
  4. Explanation two, reliability thresholds (his off-the-cuff numbers): an email writer helps at 90% right; at 50% it wastes your time, since you fix half. For code, 80% is useless; 99% is useful.
Visuals
Smooth curve, three thresholds30:00–32:40

Sketch: the curve is invented; the thresholds are the speaker's examples. Each dot is a threshold crossed, which looks like a jump from outside.

Posts & sources
Context
  • @bcherny (2026-08-20): Boris Cherny, replying to a user: "Coding is solved, bugs are not yet solved. Fix incoming". Post ↗
Further thinking
  • So it's hard to say what the next model will unlock: the curve is predictable, but you must know each task's threshold to know which gets crossed.

Why watching AI isn't enough

Alignment

Good only when watched?

one-liner

Watching the model catches some bad behavior. Interpretability goes further: it checks whether training shaped the intended character, and finds what exists only inside, unsaid.

Key points

Golden Gate Claude and taking it seriously

Golden Gate

Turn it on always, see if it fits

one-liner

Golden Gate Claude was a check: turn the "Golden Gate Bridge" feature on always, and if every answer drifts to the bridge, the decoding was right. Safety work rests on one premise: take seriously that the technology will keep improving.

Key points
  1. Training and watching are one loop: not one team making Claudes and another finding fault. Spot a concerning pattern, change training right away.
  2. Host: Anthropic is often accused of talking up danger "for marketing reasons", to raise money.
  3. His answer: much of what it does rests on one premise: "take the idea that this technology is going to keep improving seriously." Rewind to 2024–25: if you believed Claude would soon code like a trained engineer, you'd tackle things like cybersecurity early. He thinks critics often don't truly believe the technology will keep improving; so far, progress shows no sign of slowing.

When anthropomorphizing AI is useful

Anthropomorphizing

More actor than program

one-liner

Treating the model as an "entity" or a "tool" are both wrong. But for predicting what it does, "something imitating people" beats "a program" by far. All three reasons come from training.

Key points
  1. His standard: all mental models are wrong, but some are useful. When they published "models plan", some objected to "planning"; he picks the word that most easily describes what is happening.
  2. In his words: "something that's mimicking a human is … much closer than a program."

Curiosity, and reasons for optimism

Curiosity

Deny it, or look inside

one-liner

A simple objective grew complex abilities. You can meet that with denial or with curiosity. He chooses curiosity, and he is optimistic about alignment.

Key points
  1. Denial: "it's just predicting the next word; it can't match the best specialists." More evidence makes that harder to hold.
  2. Curiosity: then how does it do it? Open it up. SQL took him years to master; later, as a side project, he got Claude to learn SQL and it beat him: "I'll never be better than Claude at SQL ever again."
  3. His most optimistic hope: learning the nature of intelligence itself. Evolution, too, is a simple objective: keep organisms that adapt. "At the end, you get jazz and podcasts."
  4. Why he came on the show: so people understand what these systems are and how they're made, and can help decide.

Shared structure, gut checks, side thoughts

Gut checks and side thoughts

Different models, similar solutions

one-liner

Models trained by different people, years apart, on different data have strikingly similar insides. They also have quick gut checks and unspoken "thoughts".

Key points
  1. Convergence: across Claude generations and some open models, the learned structure overlaps a lot. His reading: important problems often have one efficient solution, like everyone doing math in base 10.
  2. Simulation hypothesis (Host asks): things settling into the same "basin" makes it believable; but he can't say who runs it or why, so he doesn't fully buy it.
  3. Jev (swyx's question, via the host): a TypeSafe AI model launched 2026-09 that quickly picks one of a developer's given options.16 It reminds him of System 1 / System 2 (fast intuition / slow deliberation): a roadmap needs long thought; "is this reviewer happy or upset?" needs only a gut call. Such models will likely be part of intelligent systems.
Visuals
Two odd findings59:00–1:02:40
Gut checkBeyond normal computation, a separate "something's wrong" mechanism: if "4 + 5 = 23" appears mid-reasoning, it flags it, since 4 + 5 is definitely under 10
Thinking on the sideAsk it to do one task while thinking of another: that thing shows up inside, not in the output. No training reason to learn this, he says: "really mysterious"
How was "thinking on the side" studied later? What is J-space?In 2026-07 Anthropic found a small inner space whose contents the model can report.

Context J-space is named after the math tool used, the Jacobian: a small inner representation that holds only a few dozen concepts at a time, under a tenth of all activity.17

ExperimentResult
Copy a sentence while thinking of citrus"orange" appears in J-space, not in the output
Swap "Spanish" for "French"Asked what language the passage is in, it says French; asked to continue, it still writes Spanish
Delete J-spaceFluent speech and simple facts barely change; multi-step reasoning drops to almost zero

The authors note: this concerns only "access consciousness", and says nothing about whether Claude has experiences.17

Getting into interpretability

Getting in

A new field of a few hundred people

one-liner

A growing but new field of a few hundred people. The on-ramp: self-study of key papers, then experiments on open platforms like Neuronpedia.

Key points
  1. Size: "maybe hundreds, not thousands", spread across the big labs and quite a few academic labs.
  2. Backgrounds: surprisingly many ex-neuroscientists. The field is so new it needs little prior knowledge.
  3. Getting started: self-study and key papers; Neuronpedia hosts a set of models that anyone can experiment on.20
  4. His close: read about how models work; if you want to know something not yet figured out, find him on X.
